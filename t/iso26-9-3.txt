A company is opening a new branch, and its quality reports are ready.
Delay complaints are rising, and an action from the previous review remains open.
Top management reviews the QMS at planned intervals for continuing suitability, adequacy, effectiveness and alignment with strategic direction.
Suitability: the system fits the organization’s work and current context.
Adequacy: its arrangements cover what is needed.
Effectiveness: it achieves intended results; expansion also calls for reviewing alignment with strategic direction.
These angles aid understanding; review judgments need actual information, not impressions.

Let us simulate a management-review meeting in this service company.
The general manager asks whether the system can maintain service levels as the new branch opens.
Quality presents delay-complaint trends and an unfinished action; operations explains workload allocation and team capacity.
A meeting is a practical review method; participants depend on need, with no prescribed list of job titles or single meeting format.
Quality: the previous action is partly implemented; there is not yet enough evidence of effectiveness.
Operations: review delayed orders and branch capacity before assuming that more staff is the solution.
The general manager asks for links to service objectives, customer needs, and expansion risks and opportunities.
Discussion brings departments’ information together to support decisions, rather than merely approving reports.
