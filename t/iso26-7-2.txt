A buyer attended training but chose the cheapest offer despite unmet specifications, causing delay and a repeat purchase.
The issue is not a missing attendance certificate: the required ability did not appear in actual work.
Clause 7.2 connects competence to QMS performance: define requirements, establish competence, address gaps and make suitable evidence available.
Start with the actual task: comparing offers requires understanding specifications, detecting exceptions and justifying selection.
Define competence appropriate to responsibility and risk, including relevant technical knowledge, system skills and professional judgment.
Job descriptions and competence matrices are useful tools, not prescribed formats or fixed course lists for every role.
The requirement covers people working under organizational control whose work affects QMS performance, beyond permanent employees.
Include temporary personnel and contractors performing relevant work under organizational control, according to the task and its impact.
HR may organize records; process owners help define needed skills and appropriate specialists contribute to assessment.
Establish competence on the basis of appropriate education, training or experience; the same combination is not required for every job.
Qualifications, experience and course records can support the judgment; consider their relevance to the task and current requirements.
For the buyer, use an appropriate real or simulated comparison and discuss the decision, rather than relying only on years of service.
Define three observable abilities: checking specification conformity, verifying delivery conditions and recording a justified selection.
Use hypothetical offers including a cheaper nonconforming offer, then observe how the buyer detects and handles the issue.
Set acceptance criteria before assessment; a high total must not hide a critical failure. This method is an example, not ISO-prescribed scoring.
Assessment shows good comparison records but a missed critical technical exception: target that specific gap.
Actions may include practical training, mentoring, reassignment, hiring or contracting competent people, as appropriate.
Match the action to the cause and risk, assign ownership and timing, and apply suitable review of sensitive decisions during learning.
The purchasing manager and technical specialist define the gap and arrange coaching on varied cases before independent performance.
Agree on assessment criteria, reassessment timing and evidence of acquired ability, rather than recording only a training activity.
Choose the number of cases and coaching duration according to need; they are not mandatory ISO thresholds.
After coaching, use a fresh case with a different exception and observe detection, clarification and a justified decision without inappropriate help.
If assessment criteria are met, record the result and evidence, then follow workplace application according to risk.
If performance has not improved, review the cause and adapt the action or responsibility; course completion does not close the gap.
Make appropriate documented information available as evidence of competence, such as relevant qualifications, practical assessment and action results.
Our example record identifies the task, criteria, assessment case, result, assessor, date and any required follow-up.
Control and protect the evidence as documented information; avoid unnecessary personal data or assuming one mandatory file format for everyone.
Competence is not a permanent judgment: a new purchasing system, material type or customer requirement may create a new skill need.
Review needs when relevant changes occur and determine suitable learning or assessment, rather than automatically repeating the same annual training.
Keep ability appropriate to the current task, while considering other obligations applicable to the organization.
Resources provide people and time, competence establishes ability, and organizational knowledge supports application and sharing.
In purchasing, competence supports external-provider control through understanding requirements and evaluating offers against them.
Track relevant process outcomes, such as repeat purchases due to comparison errors; analyze causes before attributing every failure to the employee.
