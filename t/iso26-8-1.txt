A distributor shipped on time, but a similar-looking item was picked: the quantity was right and the product was wrong.
Speed alone did not achieve conformity because picking and verification were not adequately controlled.
Clause 8.1 links planning, implementation and control of operations to product and service requirements and actions determined in Clause 6.
Before mapping steps, determine product and service requirements: item reference, quantity, delivery time and location, plus applicable packaging or other conditions.
Our hypothetical order is for 100 units of a specified item; a similar available item is not automatically an acceptable substitute.
Resolve ambiguity and handle requirements and changes through suitable arrangements, then plan operations from a clear order rather than assumptions.
Acceptance criteria define a conforming outcome, such as correct item, quantity, condition and agreed delivery conditions.
Process criteria define how a step is controlled, such as checking the item reference against the picking order before release for dispatch.
This distinction matters: describing the required result alone does not ensure that operations consistently produce it.
Picking uses a clear item reference, followed by suitable verification before dispatch, manually or through an appropriate system.
Define who verifies, when, and how discrepancies are handled; correct data elsewhere does not justify passing a mismatched item.
Choose verification method and extent according to product, risks and requirements; the example does not prescribe universal 100% inspection.
Determine resources needed for conformity: people, competence, time, facilities, transport and operational information.
Having 100 units in stock does not establish delivery capability if picking or transport capacity is unavailable when needed.
Connect this to resources and competence, addressing gaps before relying on the operational arrangement.
If risk analysis identifies similar packaging, implement action in operations, such as clearer location identification or suitable verification.
Connect the objective of reducing nonconforming shipments to the control point and its results, rather than a detached objectives display.
Planning becomes action; review whether the action reduces errors without creating overlooked problems.
A requested quantity increase after picking is a planned change requiring control: review requirements, capacity, verification and delivery effects.
After an authorized decision, update necessary information and inform affected people so picking and transport use consistent quantities.
Apply controls proportionate to the change, rather than allowing exceptions to bypass conformity safeguards.
A vehicle breakdown after picking is an unintended change: review consequences for timing, product condition and relevant service requirements.
Take necessary action to mitigate adverse effects, such as a verified suitable alternative or an authorized adjustment, with appropriate communication.
Any available vehicle is insufficient if it fails required transport conditions; base the response on actual consequences.
When transport is externally provided, ensure relevant external services are controlled, linking to clause 8.4.
Communicate requirements, choose controls appropriate to transport impact and monitor relevant capability, performance and outcomes.
Outsourcing execution does not remove accountability for the result supplied to the customer.
Make documented information available as needed for confidence that processes ran as planned, and as evidence of product and service conformity.
A verification record linked to the order may serve both purposes if it shows the activity and result; two separate files are not automatically required.
Control this information under documented-information requirements; a bare “completed” status may not be sufficient for the work’s needs.
For each step, define inputs, process and acceptance criteria, execution and verification roles, needed resources and evidence.
Walk one order through the whole plan and test a wrong-item or transport-breakdown scenario to check whether people know the response.
The plan may be in a system, linked instructions or a suitable table; clause 8.1 does not mandate one quality-plan form for every order.
Ask more than whether a procedure exists: are requirements clear, resources suitable, criteria applied, changes controlled and evidence available?
