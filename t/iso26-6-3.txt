An appliance company moves after-sales service from scattered files to one system, but some open requests are not migrated.
Staff use the new system while customers await responses to requests the team cannot see.
The problem is a change whose consequences and interfaces were not reviewed before launch: the focus of clause 6.3.
QMS changes are implemented in a planned manner; the 2026 edition specifies seven connected considerations.
Consider purpose and consequences, potential impact on system integrity, resources and information, and responsibilities and authorities.
Also consider communication, monitoring and evaluating effectiveness, and reviewing results. Pilots and fallback plans are optional implementation tools.
The purpose is fewer lost requests and faster follow-up, rather than simply buying new software.
Define scope across intake, technician assignment, tracking and closure, including open requests and data needed for continuity.
Define success criteria and review timing; quality objectives in 6.2 help assess the intended benefit after implementation.
QMS integrity means the change does not lose a requirement, check or responsibility, and connected parts continue working together.
Review information flow from customer to coordinator and technician, parts information for the warehouse, and service feedback to the customer.
Connect this to 4.4 and test the complete process rather than merely whether a software screen opens.
Unverified migration may lose an open request or change its status, breaking follow-up while the company’s commitment remains.
Use 6.1 to identify risks and select controls such as reconciling open requests, testing varied cases and checking access permissions.
Also review customer requirements and privacy implications of channel changes, considering applicable service requirements.
Identify who migrates and verifies data, training and support time, and the resources and information needed for the transition.
Information includes open requests, customer commitments and accurate status; working equipment cannot compensate for missing data.
Check team capability to operate the system and handle errors, choosing a launch date that fits workload and actual capacity.
The service manager leads the change, the system administrator configures and migrates, and the coordinator checks requests and commitments.
Define who authorizes or postpones launch, corrects data and informs customers when service arrangements are affected.
Link these assigned or revised responsibilities and authorities to 5.3 so decisions do not stall between departments.
A limited pilot may precede rollout, with acceptance criteria proportionate to change risks.
Check open-request completeness and status, permissions, routing, and staff handling of routine and exceptional cases.
If a significant issue remains, correct and retest rather than approve solely because the planned date has arrived; the authorized role decides.
Define how requests are received during transition and prevent duplicate entry across channels without reconciliation.
If a critical function fails, use an appropriate planned fallback that preserves new requests and reconciles them after recovery.
Restoring old data without accounting for later requests may create new loss, so test fallback arrangements, ownership and limits.
Update affected instructions, permissions and forms, identifying the current version and how staff access it.
Link this to documented-information control in 7.5 so old-system instructions do not remain in use.
Communicate relevant changes to affected parties through suitable channels; successful transition needs understood working methods as well as technology.
Plan how change effectiveness will be monitored and evaluated, and how results will be reviewed: explicit considerations in clause 6.3.
After launch, assess request follow-up, data accuracy, response time and complaints against success criteria over a suitable period.
Responsible people review results and unintended effects and decide actions, linking to evaluation and improvement while retaining applicable design and service-change controls.
Before a QMS change, determine purpose, consequences and integrity impact; ensure resources, information, responsibilities and authorities are considered.
Plan communication, effectiveness monitoring and evaluation, and results review, using transition methods that preserve service commitments.
