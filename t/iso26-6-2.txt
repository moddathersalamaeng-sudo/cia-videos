A fit-out contractor works overtime and holds many meetings, yet inspection requests return with preventable defects.
Meeting counts describe activity, but do not alone show whether work conforms at the first inspection.
We need a clear objective, a suitable indicator and an implementation plan: the subject of clause 6.2.
Set quality objectives at relevant functions, levels and processes, consistent with the quality policy, measurable and considering applicable requirements.
Objectives address product and service conformity and enhanced customer satisfaction; monitor, communicate and update them as appropriate, retaining documented information.
Plan actions, resources, responsibility, completion timing and evaluation of results; announcing a target alone is insufficient.
The policy commits to quality execution, while risk review shows repeated handover without adequate internal checks.
Connect this to improving first-inspection acceptance, then define how execution, review and coordination contribute.
Policy in 5.2 and risks in 6.1 connect to objectives, processes and responsibilities, rather than arbitrary numbers.
An objective describes the intended result; a Key Performance Indicator, or KPI, measures an important aspect of progress towards it.
Our KPI is first-inspection acceptance rate; the target is the value we aim to reach within a defined period.
Checklists and training are actions, not automatic evidence of success. Not every tracked metric is a key indicator.
SMART helps make an objective Specific and Measurable, so the intended change and its measurement are clear.
It should also be Achievable with available capability and resources, Relevant to quality results, and Time-bound.
SMART is an optional formulation tool, not a required ISO label, and does not replace clause requirements, planning or monitoring.
Hypothetical objective: increase first-inspection acceptance of fit-out inspection requests from 80% to 95% by the end of the next quarter.
Define included projects and request types; assess feasibility using rejection causes, capability and resources.
Link the objective to the policy and handover requirements while preserving all acceptance criteria; targets do not justify passing nonconforming work.
Calculate requests accepted at first inspection divided by all requests whose first inspection was completed in the period, multiplied by 100.
With 40 accepted out of 50, baseline performance is 80%; use a clear acceptance definition and consistent data source.
Keep rejected requests in the denominator and do not count corrected resubmissions as new requests; define frequency and data ownership.
The plan analyses rejection causes, updates the checklist, trains the team on recurring defects and verifies work before submission.
Allocate review time and suitable inspection resources; the project manager leads delivery with the quality supervisor, using milestones and a completion date.
Evaluate monthly and at quarter end, linking actions, resources, responsibility, timing and evaluation under clause 6.2.2.
First-inspection acceptance is an outcome indicator; internal verification before submission can provide an earlier signal about execution.
A high execution measure does not guarantee better outcomes; review the quality of checks and recurring defect causes.
Also monitor waiting time and overdue requests so acceptance does not improve artificially through delaying difficult cases or hiding failures.
