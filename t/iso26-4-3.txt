A contractor claims its quality system covers all its work, but implementation only covers building fit-outs.
Customers may assume design and structural construction are included too. That creates a misleading impression.
Clause 4.3 helps define the quality management system scope clearly so the statement reflects reality.
Start with internal and external context, relevant interested-party requirements, and the products and services provided.
Review these inputs with operational owners, not only the quality manager.
Then determine the system boundaries and applicability rather than copying another company’s certificate.
Our fictional contractor delivers office fit-outs through an administrative office, a warehouse and project sites.
Map where work is planned, materials are stored, and the service is delivered and handed over.
Ignoring project sites would hide the place where service quality becomes visible to customers.
Trace the service from reviewing the customer request through purchasing, execution, inspection and handover.
Identify supporting processes that affect the result, such as competent staffing and equipment maintenance.
Scope is not a selection of convenient departments; its boundaries must cover what is needed to deliver the agreed service.
The company uses an external contractor for painting work within the project.
External execution does not remove responsibility for conformity of the service promised to the customer.
Define specifications, select a suitable contractor, monitor delivery and verify the result through external-provider controls.
A company executing a complete customer-supplied design may not be responsible for service design, but contracts and actual practice must be examined.
If it develops the solution or changes its specifications under its responsibility, calling itself execution-only is insufficient.
Non-applicability needs justification and must not affect the ability or responsibility to provide conforming services and enhance customer satisfaction.
For example: execution and handover of office interior fit-outs, managed through the office and warehouse, at project sites identified in the site register.
Link this statement to actual sites, activity boundaries and responsibilities, and document any justified non-applicability.
This is an educational example, not a statement to copy without checking your organization’s reality.
The QMS scope defines what the management system covers within the organization.
The certification scope identifies what is actually certified; an audit scope defines the extent, activities and locations of a particular audit.
These scopes may differ. Customer-facing claims must not suggest that certification of a limited activity covers everything.
Take a live project and compare its contract with the locations and activities covered by the scope.
Trace materials purchasing, external-contractor work, inspection and handover, checking responsibilities.
If a relevant activity is uncontrolled or a location unclear, correct the boundary or interface controls rather than merely polishing the wording.
Maintain the scope as available documented information, including product and service types and justification of non-applicability.
When adding design services, a branch or a new project type, review effects on scope, processes and responsibilities.
In our example, operations and quality review the change, updating documents and customer communications before announcing expansion.
Compare “all contracting activities” with “execution and handover of office interior fit-outs at identified sites.”
The second is more accurate for our example because it describes a real service and verifiable boundaries.
Accurate wording alone is insufficient: implementation and relevant interfaces must match it.
Start with products and services, identify sites and boundaries, then review applicability and responsibility for external work.
Write the scope, retain reasons for any non-applicability, and test it against a real order or project before approval.
