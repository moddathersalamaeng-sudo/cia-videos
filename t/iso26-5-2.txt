An accounting-services office displays its quality policy, but staff do not know what to do when data arrives incomplete before a report deadline.
The wall says quality comes first, while daily pressure encourages sending the report anyway.
A policy matters when it guides decisions, not merely when it looks attractive in a frame.
The policy sets quality direction and commitments; an objective defines a result to achieve and measure.
A procedure explains how work is performed, such as reviewing and approving reports before issue.
Do not overload the policy with every step or reduce it to a slogan that cannot guide objectives.
Top management establishes, implements and maintains a policy suited to the organization’s purpose and context and supporting strategic direction.
It provides a framework for quality objectives and includes a commitment to applicable requirements.
It also commits to continual improvement of the QMS; customer satisfaction or product quality alone is not the whole policy.
Our fictional office prepares accounting reports and depends on accurate information, clear communication and agreed commitments.
Review services, customer and applicable regulatory requirements, and team capability to identify what the policy should reflect.
If the wording could fit any business unchanged, examine its connection to the organization’s purpose and direction.
We commit to accurate accounting-report services delivered within agreed times, supporting our direction as a dependable partner to customers.
We commit to applicable requirements and use these commitments as a framework for setting and monitoring quality objectives.
We continually improve the QMS through staff competence, process review and customer feedback.
Take the commitment to accuracy and connect it to reducing reports requiring correction due to office errors.
Define baseline, target, period, owner and measurement in the objectives plan, linked to checks before report issue.
Targets depend on the organization. Detailed objective planning is covered in clause 6.2.
Clause 5.2 requires the policy to be communicated, understood and applied within the organization, not merely published.
Explain each team’s contribution using work examples rather than demanding word-for-word recitation.
Report preparers should know what to check, when to clarify and how to respond when deadlines conflict with complete information.
Meeting a deadline does not mean sending a report that conceals the effect of missing data.
Assess the missing information and clarify requirements and appropriate delivery options with the customer, consistent with the agreement and applicable requirements.
Record the decision as needed: this tests application of the policy rather than memorization.
Keep the quality policy available and maintained as documented information so people can access the current version.
Control approval, updates and access through suitable document arrangements, avoiding obsolete versions that conflict with current direction.
It may be electronic or printed; what matters is a clear, available and maintained reference.
Make the policy available to relevant interested parties as appropriate.
The office might provide it to customers on request or through a suitable access channel.
This does not universally require public website publication or sending it to everyone; choose arrangements suited to the parties and relationships.
When services, direction or significant requirements change, review whether the policy remains suitable.
Do not force an annual rewrite or keep wording unchanged despite a changed business; base the decision on suitability.
Check alignment between policy, objectives and actual processes, updating communication when needed.
Draft a policy aligned with purpose, context and direction, including an objectives framework, applicable requirements and continual QMS improvement.
Engage top management, test understanding through a work scenario, and check documentation, availability and application.
