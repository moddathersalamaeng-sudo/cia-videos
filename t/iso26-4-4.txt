A new employee arrives, but the computer, access permissions and onboarding plan are not ready.
Each department says it did its part, yet the intended result was not achieved.
The problem lies in how work connects across departments: clause 4.4, the quality management system and its processes.
A process uses connected activities to transform inputs into intended outputs.
Onboarding starts with employee details, a start date and role requirements, and ends with verified readiness to work.
Sending an email is an activity, but alone it does not prove employee readiness.
Map the employee journey across HR, IT and the line manager.
Each handoff needs clear information, a due date and suitable evidence of receipt or completion.
This reveals gaps hidden by departmental task lists and makes process sequence and interaction visible.
A simple process card can capture purpose, inputs, outputs, ownership, resources, controls and measures.
Connect it to preceding and following processes and the risks and opportunities to address.
The card or diagram is optional; the requirement is to determine, implement, control and improve processes, not merely draw them.
In this example, readiness means a prepared device, approved role-appropriate access, an agreed onboarding plan and manager verification.
Define how and when to verify readiness before the start date, with suitable people, equipment and systems available.
These are example operating criteria, not a universal checklist imposed by ISO 9001.
Assign process responsibilities and authorities, including who follows the result and escalates delays.
In our example, an onboarding coordinator tracks completion while departments deliver their tasks and authorized owners approve decisions.
Process ownership does not transfer every task to one person; it prevents the outcome from being lost between departments.
Fast access setup is not success if permissions exceed the role’s needs.
Address incomplete data and late preparation with controls such as a common request and role-based approval.
Consider system integration to reduce duplicate entry, then evaluate the effectiveness of these actions.
Measure on-time verified readiness against all employees due to start during the period.
In fictional data, 16 of 20 were ready on time: 80 percent.
Also check approval completeness so speed does not undermine controls. Targets are set for the organization, not prescribed by ISO.
Reviewing the four late cases revealed late role information in three of them.
The team changed the onboarding request to collect required data early and assigned completeness checks before submission.
After implementation, compare results over a suitable period, confirming sustained improvement and checking for new problems.
Maintain the documented information needed to support process operation, such as onboarding steps and acceptance criteria where needed.
Retain evidence that work was carried out as planned, such as approvals, preparation records and verification.
Documentation should suit complexity, risk and competence; every step does not need a lengthy procedure.
Processes are connected: one output becomes another input, and the final result depends on those links.
Plan resources and criteria, do the work, evaluate performance, and improve processes and the system as a whole.
Clause 4.4 becomes a daily way of managing results rather than a diagram on the wall.
